# Mistake Notes

## 1. Not Understanding the Judge's Role

### User Observation

Teams should tailor their pitch to the judge the same way a candidate tailors a
resume to a job. A non-technical judge needs the problem, solution, and impact.
A backend judge may appreciate architecture, data flow, and technical tradeoffs.
A frontend judge may care more about user experience, polish, and interaction.

### Supporting Research

- Audience analysis guidance recommends understanding audience goals,
  challenges, knowledge level, and objections before presenting:
  [Audience Analysis for Presentations](https://slidecoach.app/audience-analysis-for-presentations/).
- Audience-awareness guidance says the amount of background and terminology
  depends on what the audience already knows:
  [Audience Awareness](https://writingcenter.tamu.edu/guides/resources/audience-awareness.html).

### Blog Takeaway

The goal is not to fake expertise. The goal is to make the best parts of the
project obvious to the person scoring it.

## 2. Having Everyone on the Team Pitch

### User Observation

In a four-person team, one person is usually the strongest presenter and one is
usually the weakest. If the judge cannot understand a teammate, the team loses
clarity and may waste Q&A time repeating information.

### Supporting Research

- Group presentation guidance recommends choosing a confident team captain to
  open, close, manage transitions, and field questions:
  [How to Present Better as a Group](https://secondnature.com.au/blog/pitching-and-new-business/team-presentations-how-to-present-better-as-a-group/).
- Group presentation guidance emphasizes one message, one flow, and one level of
  confidence:
  [How To Present As A Group](https://benjaminball.com/blog/how-to-present-as-a-group-presentations/).

### Blog Takeaway

The pitch is judged on clarity, not fairness. Let the strongest communicator
lead and let specialists answer deeper questions later.

## 3. Delivering the Pitch With No Energy

### User Observation

Teams forget to smile or speak with energy. Judges feed off the presenter's
energy. If the pitch is monotone, the judge has to work harder to understand
what is exciting.

### Supporting Research

- Research on vocal smiles describes emotional contagion as a process where
  people infer and react to emotional states through sensory, motor,
  physiological, and affective responses:
  [Emotional contagion to vocal smile](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-74848-w).
- Research on smile mimicry shows that people mimic smiles in audio-visual
  communication contexts:
  [Smile Mimicry and Emotional Contagion](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02077/full).

### Blog Takeaway

Energy does not replace substance, but it changes how memorable the substance
feels.

## 4. Using Slides Like a Script

### User Observation

Slides should have no more than about 10 to 15 words. Paragraphs force judges
to choose between reading the slide and listening to the speaker. Slides should
frame the problem with statistics, photos, and small bullets, then get out of
the way. Product screenshots should usually become demos instead.

### Supporting Research

- Evidence-based presentation guidance cites the redundancy principle: people
  learn better from graphics and narration than from graphics, narration, and
  printed text:
  [Evidence-Based Presentation Design Recommendations](https://multimedia.ucsd.edu/best-practices/presentation-design.html).
- Research on redundant text in multimedia learning found that sparse or
  complementary text can outperform redundant text:
  [Learners misperceive the benefits of redundant text in multimedia learning](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00710/full).

### Blog Takeaway

Slides are for framing. The product demo is for proof.

## 5. Explaining Concepts the Judge Already Knows

### User Observation

Some teams spent 30 seconds explaining OpenAI Agents or ElevenLabs Agents even
when the judge already knew those tools. In a one or two minute pitch, that is
too much time to lose. A simple calibration question solves it: "Have you used
this before?"

### Supporting Research

- Public speaking guidance says prior knowledge strongly affects what an
  audience learns and that presenters should avoid both boring experts with
  basics and confusing beginners by skipping fundamentals:
  [Informing the Audience](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Public_Speaking/Public_Speaking_\(Lumen_Learning\)/09%3A_Informative_Speaking/9.03%3A_Informing_the_Audience).
- Audience-awareness guidance recommends deciding how much background and which
  terms need explanation based on audience knowledge:
  [Audience Awareness](https://writingcenter.tamu.edu/guides/resources/audience-awareness.html).

### Blog Takeaway

The pitch is not a tutorial. Ask what the judge knows, then spend the saved time
on what your team actually built.

## 6. Pitching Every Feature Instead of the Main Flow

### User Observation

Judges do not need to hear about every landing page, auth page, or database
schema unless it is central to the problem. The pitch should be the problem,
then the demo should show how the solution solves that problem through the main
user flow.

### Supporting Research

- Hackathon presentation guidance recommends focusing on problem, solution,
  demo, and impact:
  [How to Make a Presentation for a Hackathon](https://slidemodel.com/hackathon-presentation/).
- Hackathon presentation advice emphasizes concise structure and a focused
  demonstration of the key product value:
  [How to Make a Presentation for a Hackathon - Easy Guide](https://slidestack.com/blog/how-to-make-a-presentation-for-a-hackathon-easy-guide).

### Blog Takeaway

A pitch is not a feature inventory. It is a guided path through the project
value.

## 7. Not Having a Backup Demo Video

### User Observation

LA Hacks judging Wi-Fi was spotty. Some teams could not demo and the judge had
no clear idea what the project did. A backup video also helps on Devpost during
deliberation, when judges may need to remember the project.

### Supporting Research

- Devpost recommends recording a demo video that showcases key features and
  explains the problem, project, and impact:
  [6 Tips for making a winning hackathon demo video](https://info.devpost.com/blog/6-tips-for-making-a-hackathon-demo-video).
- Devpost video best practices recommend opening with what the app does, using
  a screencast, editing messy parts, and uploading early:
  [Video-making best practices](https://help.devpost.com/hc/en-us/articles/360021816952-Video-making-best-practices).
- Devpost judging guidance explains that judges rate submissions through a
  dashboard against criteria:
  [How to judge an online Hackathon](https://help.devpost.com/article/103-how-to-judge-an-online-hackathon).

### Blog Takeaway

The backup video is not optional polish. It is insurance against network,
device, and memory failures.

## 8. Not Having an Appendix

### User Observation

Judges often ask follow-up questions if time allows. Teams should have appendix
slides for architecture, prompt examples, future mockups, research data, and
other deeper information that makes them look knowledgeable.

### Supporting Research

- DECKO describes the appendix as the place for important but non-essential
  details that would overcomplicate the main deck:
  [What goes in a pitch deck's appendix?](https://www.getdecko.com/deckhammer/appendix).
- Appendix guidance says supplementary slides help answer detailed questions,
  maintain narrative focus, and demonstrate rigor:
  [Appendix Slide PowerPoint](https://deckary.com/blog/appendix-slide-powerpoint).
- Pitch deck appendix guidance says the appendix should contain information
  that strengthens credibility but interrupts the main story:
  [How to Make the Pitch Deck Appendix](https://www.inknarrates.com/post/pitch-deck-appendix).

### Blog Takeaway

The main pitch should be simple. The appendix should make the team look
prepared when the judge goes deeper.

## 9. Not Leaving Enough Time for Q&A

### User Observation

At LA Hacks, the judging slot was five minutes, with about 1.5 minutes intended
for pitch and demo and about 3.5 minutes for questions. Many teams ran long,
and some spent almost the whole slot pitching. When judges cannot ask questions,
they may lack the information needed to score rubric categories.

### Supporting Research

- SCORE's pitch contest FAQ uses a structured time split, with 10 minutes for
  the pitch and 5 minutes for Q&A:
  [SCORE Tech Entrepreneur Pitch Contest FAQs](https://score.org/seattle/tech-entrepreneur-pitch-contest/faqs).
- Entrepreneur pitch competition advice recommends anticipating judge questions
  and using the pitch to address important basics:
  [The Do's and Don'ts of Acing a Pitch Competition](https://www.entrepreneur.com/starting-a-business/the-dos-and-donts-of-acing-a-pitch-competition/249562).

### Blog Takeaway

Q&A is not leftover time. It is where the judge fills in missing rubric
information and often decides the final score.
