# Blog Engagement Research: Why Readers Finish (or Don't)

**Read this before any substantial blog rewrite or new long-form post.** It is the evidence base behind how we structure articles on this site. The companion `docs/blog-engagement-checklist.md` turns it into a pre-publish checklist; this file is the "why."

Every claim here is sourced. The statistics were independently fact-checked, and the corrections are baked in (see "Stats to cite carefully" at the end for the ones people commonly get wrong). Follow the site's no-em-dash and full-attribution rules when you reuse any of this in published content.

---

## The 8 levers, ranked by leverage

1. **Front-load everything (inverted pyramid).** Readers absorb only about 20 to 28% of a page's words ([NN/g, 2008](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/)) and spend about 57% of viewing time above the fold ([NN/g, 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)). Put the answer, claim, or hook in the first sentence.
2. **Win the first screen.** About 38% of arrivals bounce immediately, and across the Chartbeat network the median reader stops near the halfway mark ([Slate / Chartbeat, 2013](https://slate.com/technology/2013/06/how-people-read-online-why-you-wont-finish-this-article.html)). 55% of pageviews get under 15 seconds of active attention ([Time / Chartbeat, 2014](https://time.com/12933/what-you-think-you-know-about-the-web-is-wrong/)).
3. **Spend your best material before the 50% mark.** The back half of an article is read by a minority, so treat it as depth for committed readers, not as where the payoff lives.
4. **Cut verbosity.** Word count is not a Google ranking factor, per Google's John Mueller ([Search Engine Journal, 2020](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/word-count-not-a-quality-factor/397288/)). Longer pages correlate with rankings only because they tend to be more comprehensive. Edit each sentence until no word can be removed ([Julian Shapiro, 2021](https://www.julian.com/guide/write/rewriting)).
5. **Engineer momentum sentence by sentence (the "slippery slide").** Each element's only job is to get the next one read ([Joseph Sugarman, *Advertising Secrets of the Written Word*, 1998](https://thisiscopy.com/joseph-sugarmans-copywriting-approach/)). Make the opening sentence trivially short.
6. **Open small, concrete curiosity gaps and pay them off.** Curiosity is strongest when the gap is specific and the reader already knows enough to feel it, not vague ([George Loewenstein, 1994](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/golman_loewenstein_curiosity.pdf)).
7. **Make it scannable.** One newspaper lifted average engaged time about 20% (51s to over 60s) just by adding subheadings at the measured drop-off point ([Chartbeat, 2020](https://blog.chartbeat.com/2020/03/09/content-scroll-depth-use-case/)).
8. **Measure consumption, not reach.** Shares and pageviews barely correlate with how deeply a piece is read ([Slate / Chartbeat, 2013](https://slate.com/technology/2013/06/how-people-read-online-why-you-wont-finish-this-article.html)). Pair scroll depth with engaged time.

## 1. Why readers drop off

**The default is to leave, not to read.** The writer's real job is mechanical: make the cost of stopping feel higher than the cost of reading one more line.

**Attention dies high on the page.** Eye-tracking found about 57% of viewing time is spent above the fold and about 74% within the first two screenfuls; more than 42% lands in the top 20% of the page ([NN/g, 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)). Jakob Nielsen's metaphor from the original study: readers arrive with "fuel in their tanks" that depletes as they scroll, so the top rows draw far more fixations than lower ones ([NN/g, 2010](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention-original-research/)).

**Most words go unread.** On an average visit, users have time to read at most 28% of a page's words, with about 20% the realistic figure. Nielsen's model is roughly 25 seconds of fixed overhead plus 4.4 seconds per additional 100 words at 250 wpm, which means readers cross the 50%-read threshold only on pages of about 111 words or fewer ([NN/g, 2008](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/)).

**They scan in an F-pattern.** A horizontal sweep across the top, a shorter sweep lower down, then a vertical scan down the left margin. The pattern emerges when text is unformatted and motivation to read everything is low, so anything not front-loaded falls into the dead zones ([NN/g, 2017](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)).

**The mechanism is cognitive load.** Working memory holds only a handful of chunks at once, so dense formatting, long sentences, and jargon impose extraneous load that crowds out comprehension ([ReadabilityMatters, 2021](https://readabilitymatters.org/articles/increase-readability-reduce-cognitive-load)). Cutting load frees capacity for the reader to grasp and retain the argument.

**Speed is a precondition.** 53% of mobile visits are abandoned when a page takes longer than 3 seconds to load ([Marketing Dive / Google, 2017](https://www.marketingdive.com/news/google-53-of-mobile-users-abandon-sites-that-take-over-3-seconds-to-load/426070/)). If the post is slow, none of the writing advice matters.

## 2. Structure and scannability

**Inverted pyramid.** Lead with the conclusion, then reasoning, then background, so a reader who quits after one sentence still gets the point ([NN/g, Amy Schade, 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/inverted-pyramid/)).

**Front-load information-carrying words.** Start headings, subheadings, paragraphs, and even sentences with the words that carry meaning, never "In order to" or "There are several." This gives the scanning eye high-value entry points ([NN/g, 2017](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)).

**The slippery slide.** The headline's only job is to earn the first sentence; each sentence's only job is to earn the next ([Joseph Sugarman, 1998](https://thisiscopy.com/joseph-sugarmans-copywriting-approach/)). Make the opening sentence almost trivially short ("It started with a rejection email.") so momentum starts at near-zero cognitive cost.

**Bucket brigades.** Short standalone transitional lines whose only purpose is to move the reader to the next line: "Here's the thing:", "But it gets worse:", "Still not convinced?". Drop one wherever momentum sags, and end on a colon or open question so the next line is the only resolution ([Joanna Wiebe, Copyhackers, 2020](https://copyhackers.com/engage-bucket-brigade-technique/)).

**Curiosity gaps.** Curiosity arises from a perceived gap between what you know and what you want to know, and it feels like a deprivation the reader is driven to resolve. The gap must be small and concrete ("There's one mistake that quietly kills most hackathon pitches"), and you must always pay it off; unresolved loops breed frustration ([George Loewenstein, 1994](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/golman_loewenstein_curiosity.pdf)).

**The trifecta of intrigue.** A compelling piece needs three beats: a captivating intro that promises novelty and usefulness, at least one peak of genuine surprise in the middle, and an ending that retroactively justifies the read. If the middle has no surprise, attention leaks no matter how strong the hook ([Julian Shapiro, 2021](https://www.julian.com/guide/write/intro)).

**Thesis, antithesis, synthesis.** Open with the belief the reader holds, dismantle it with the strongest counter-evidence, then resolve into your new idea. The tension itself is a curiosity gap that pulls toward the synthesis ([Animalz, 2021](https://www.animalz.co/blog/content-writing-guide)).

**Layer evidence types.** Rotate story, then data, then expert quote, then a fresh metaphor for the same point. Each switch changes the texture of the page and re-engages a reader about to bounce ([Animalz, 2021](https://www.animalz.co/blog/content-writing-guide)).

**Reward the finisher.** Replace "In conclusion, we covered" with a forward payoff: one concrete next step, a sharper restatement through a new image, or a bonus insight held back as the closing reward. Finishers are your most valuable readers ([Animalz, 2021](https://www.animalz.co/blog/content-writing-guide)).

## 3. Fighting verbosity

**Stop padding for SEO.** "The number of words on a page is not a quality factor, not a ranking factor," per Google's John Mueller ([Search Engine Journal, 2020](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/word-count-not-a-quality-factor/397288/)). Cover the search intent fully, then cut everything that adds no new information.

**Budget your best content for the first stretch.** Animalz's 2021 benchmark (Sept 2020 to Aug 2021, 155.7M pageviews across 60,000+ pieces on roughly 100 mostly-SaaS blogs) found a median time on page of 3 minutes 19 seconds, a 74.8% median bounce rate, and a 1,200-word median article length ([Animalz, 2021](https://www.animalz.co/blog/benchmark-report-2021)). A large share of a 1,200-word post is read by a minority, so structure later sections to stand alone for skimmers.

**Separate drafting from editing.** Write a fast, "terrible" first draft for yourself, then rewrite for the reader. Clarity, intrigue, and succinctness are products of the rewrite, not the first pass ([Julian Shapiro, 2021](https://www.julian.com/guide/write/rewriting)).

**The brevity test.** A sentence is finished only when no additional word can be removed. On the edit pass, ask of every statement "what is its purpose?" and delete anything that does not serve it ([Julian Shapiro, 2021](https://www.julian.com/guide/write/rewriting)).

**Cull the usual suspects.** Run a find-and-cull pass for filler and qualifiers ("very", "really", "just", "that", "in order to", "there is/are", "the fact that"), swap wordy phrases ("in order to" to "to", "the reason is that" to "because"), prefer active over passive voice, and read the draft aloud to catch padding the eye skips ([Purdue OWL](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/conciseness/eliminating_words.html); William Zinsser, *On Writing Well*).

**Match length to intent.** Length and engagement trade off. Long, comprehensive pieces earn more links and shares; short, focused posts draw more discussion. Go long for evergreen linkable topics, short and pointed for opinion. Do not inflate a simple answer to "rank better."

## 4. Interactivity that boosts completion (and what backfires)

**Active beats passive.** Chi and Wylie's ICAP framework orders cognitive engagement Interactive > Constructive > Active > Passive, with learning improving at each step ([Chi & Wylie, 2014](https://education.asu.edu/sites/g/files/litvpz656/files/lcl/chiwylie2014icap_2.pdf)). The lever is getting the reader to generate something: a 2-question knowledge check, a "predict before you reveal" expandable, a fill-in calculator.

**Scrollytelling works for the right intent.** A controlled within-subject study found scrollytelling scored significantly higher than static formatting on 9 of 11 engagement statements ([Soderstrom et al., ECCE 2023](https://digitaleconomy.wales/documents/ecce-2023-papers/06-The-Impact-of-Scrollytelling.pdf)). Caveat: it raises the feeling of engagement more than recall of specific facts, so use it for intros, concept explanations, and data stories, not for reference content where readers are hunting for a known fact.

**Progress indicators exploit the goal-gradient effect.** People accelerate as they near a goal; in a field experiment, customers given a 12-stamp card pre-stamped with 2 finished faster than those given a fresh 10-stamp card despite identical real effort ([Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng, 2006](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39)). On multi-step quizzes or guides, show a progress bar and seed visible early progress. If a bar lowers completion, the flow is too long; shorten it and A/B test.

**What backfires #1: accordions that hide core content.** Collapse-by-default sections raise interaction cost, hide content from awareness, and break narrative reading. Eye-tracking shows users do scroll well-organized content, so the "keep it short by hiding it" rationale does not hold ([NN/g, 2014](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/accordions-complex-content/)). Reserve accordions for independent FAQ items or optional deep dives. Never hide your core argument behind a click.

**What backfires #2: heavy embeds that slow the page.** A YouTube embed can add about 500KB to initial load, and third-party content is often render-blocking ([web.dev, 2023](https://web.dev/articles/embed-best-practices)). Use a facade (static thumbnail plus click-to-load), add `loading="lazy"` and explicit dimensions to iframes, and keep rich media on one canonical page, cross-linking from siblings.

**Place interactions high.** Do not bury your best quiz, calculator, or CTA at the bottom where only a minority of readers arrive. Put at least one high-value interaction in the first two screenfuls.

## 5. Measuring it: scroll depth, engaged time, and our events

**Scroll depth measures exposure, not reading.** A reader who hits 75% of a 2,000-word post in 30 seconds is scanning; one who reaches 75% in 4 minutes is reading. The honest definition of a "read" is scroll depth combined with engaged time.

**Engaged time vs time-on-page.** Engaged time counts only seconds with active scroll, mouse, keyboard, or touch events, on a rolling window per interaction. Time-on-page counts wall-clock time a tab is open, which is inflated by idle background tabs ([Chartbeat methodology, 2014](https://help.chartbeat.com/hc/en-us/articles/360045890913-User-Engagement-Tracking-Methodology)). Always label which one you are reporting.

**Benchmarks to calibrate against:**
- Median reader reaches roughly 50 to 60% scroll depth; only about 25% pass the deepest sections ([Slate / Chartbeat, 2013](https://slate.com/technology/2013/06/how-people-read-online-why-you-wont-finish-this-article.html)).
- About 57% of viewing time is above the fold, about 74% in the first two screenfuls ([NN/g, 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)).
- Industry-average scroll hovers around 50% and is declining ([Contentsquare, 2023](https://contentsquare.com/blog/average-time-spent-on-websites-is-dropping/)).
- **Read it this way:** above roughly 50 to 60% median scroll with healthy engaged time is strong; below about 40% is a red flag for a weak intro, slow load, or mismatched intent, not a length problem.

**How to read a drop-off cliff.** Plot the scroll or per-section histogram. A steep cliff at a specific depth marks the exact section where readers quit. Neue Zuercher Zeitung used a scroll-depth heat map to find its drop-off point, tested subheadings vs plain text vs bullets, and lifted average engaged time from 51 seconds to over 60 (about 20%) just by adding subheadings ([Chartbeat, 2020](https://blog.chartbeat.com/2020/03/09/content-scroll-depth-use-case/)). Intervene precisely at the cliff, do not sprinkle fixes everywhere.

### What our site already emits

`components/blog-analytics.tsx` (mounted per post by `app/blog/[slug]/page.tsx`) sends these PostHog events. The events are also tabulated in `docs/analytics-setup.md`.

| Event | When | Key properties |
|---|---|---|
| `blog_post_viewed` | On page load | `blog_slug`, `blog_title`, `blog_reading_time` |
| `blog_scroll_milestone` | At 25 / 50 / 75 / 100% page scroll | `scroll_percent`, `time_on_page_seconds`, `engaged_seconds` |
| `blog_section_viewed` | When each article section scrolls into view | `section_index`, `section_heading`, `engaged_seconds` |
| `blog_reading_completed` | Reached the end marker AND engaged 30s+ | `engaged_seconds`, `time_on_page_seconds` |
| `blog_post_engagement` | On exit (pagehide / SPA unmount, after 2s+) | `time_spent_seconds`, `engaged_seconds`, `max_scroll_percent`, `reading_completed` |

`section_index` comes from `data-blog-section` on each `<section>`; `blog_reading_completed` fires off the `<div data-blog-end>` marker. Engaged time only ticks while the tab is visible and the reader was active within the last 5 seconds.

### How to find the cliff in PostHog

1. **Per-section funnel.** Build a Funnel of `blog_section_viewed` filtered to one `blog_slug`, ordered by `section_index` (0, 1, 2, ...). The step with the biggest drop is the section readers quit at. This is the highest-value insight; it names the cliff instead of just the percentage.
2. **Scanner vs reader split.** Break `blog_post_engagement` down by `blog_slug` and compare `engaged_seconds` against `time_spent_seconds`. A high `max_scroll_percent` with low `engaged_seconds` means people are scanning, not reading.
3. **True completion rate.** Funnel `blog_post_viewed` to `blog_reading_completed`, per slug. This filters out fast scrollers that a raw 100% scroll would count.
4. **Scrollmap.** Use the PostHog toolbar Heatmap / scrollmap on a live blog URL to confirm the cliff visually.
5. **Session replay.** Watch a few replays of sessions that bailed at the cliff section to learn why (dense paragraph, confusing transition, slow embed).

Then apply the Section 2 to 4 fixes precisely at that section and re-measure against the pre-edit baseline.

## Stats to cite carefully (fact-check corrections)

The site requires full attribution on every quoted figure. These are the ones people commonly get wrong. Do not repeat them in the loose form.

- **The "8-second attention span, shorter than a goldfish" stat is fabricated.** It traces to a Microsoft Canada report citing Statistic Brain, whose own sources do not support it. Do not cite it.
- **"79% of users scan / 16% read word by word"** is real but from Jakob Nielsen's "How Users Read on the Web," 1997, not the later F-pattern article. Cite the 1997 date or skip it; it is roughly 28 years old.
- **The Slate numbers** (about 38% bounce, median around 50% scroll) are Slate-wide and Chartbeat-network aggregates from 2013, not measurements of a single article. Frame them as network aggregates with the date.
- **The Animalz "3:19 means about 400 words read"** is an unverified inference. The reported figures are the 3min 19s median time on page, 74.8% median bounce, and 1,200-word median length. Use those, drop the "400 words" equivalence.
- **The length-vs-shares figures** (for example 3.47 vs 11.07 shares by length) come from SEMrush / MarketingCharts and Moz / BuzzSumo, not Yoast as they are often attributed. Attribute to the original source.
