## The Little Alchemy Method

Start with basic elements and keep combining them until one result is worth building.

TL;DR Pair technologies with industries, many times over, and keep the few pairings that land.

In the game Little Alchemy you combine fire, water, earth, and air into steam, lava, and life, then keep combining the results into hundreds of other things.

Hackathon ideas work the same way, with technologies and industries as the elements.

#### The Adjacent Possible

> “Good ideas are not conjured out of thin air; they are built out of a collection of existing parts, the composition of which expands (and, occasionally, contracts) over time.”

Johnson calls that expanding edge the “adjacent possible,” a term he borrowed from biologist Stuart Kauffman. Every new API, model, or dataset expands it.

— Steven Johnson, science writer, [Where Good Ideas Come From, 2010](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Where_Good_Ideas_Come_From)

#### Notice, Don't Invent

> “Live in the future, then build what's missing. … The verb you want to be using with respect to startup ideas is not ‘think up’ but ‘notice.’”

— Paul Graham, Y Combinator co-founder, [How to Get Startup Ideas, 2012](https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)

TL;DR After you combine, go do something else and write down whatever comes to mind.

#### TFT Helps Me Win Hackathons

Yes, Teamfight Tactics by Riot Games.

Rapid Context Switching

TFT keeps you switching between the board and your idea list, and that back-and-forth is where unexpected pairings show up.

Built-in Downtime

Unlike Valorant or League, TFT has long pauses between rounds, perfect for jotting down ideas. Try brainstorming mid-clutch in Valorant: it doesn't work.

You don't have to play TFT. Cooking, cleaning, the gym, or a walk work too. Put the break on your schedule.

#### Focused vs. Diffuse Thinking

Barbara Oakley, professor of engineering at Oakland University, popularized two thinking modes in A Mind for Numbers (2014) and in the Coursera course [Learning How to Learn](https://www.coursera.org/learn/learning-how-to-learn) (2014), which she teaches with neuroscientist Terrence Sejnowski:

Focused Mode

Concentrated work on a known problem, like forcing ideas at a whiteboard.

Diffuse Mode

Relaxed and associative. It's why the idea hits on the walk to get coffee, or between TFT rounds.

> “Sometimes we need to lose concentration so we can think more clearly.”

— Barbara Oakley and Terrence Sejnowski with Alistair McConville, [Learning How to Learn](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/563935/learning-how-to-learn-by-barbara-oakley-phd-and-terrence-sejnowski-phd-with-alistair-mcconville/), 2018

## The Ideation Toolkit

For when random combining stops turning up new ideas.

TL;DR Run SCAMPER on your top ideas, or pick a random noun and force a connection to your problem.

#### SCAMPER Method

Education author Bob Eberle's [Scamper: Games for Imagination Development](https://archive.org/details/scampergamesfori0000eber) (1971) turned Alex Osborn's brainstorming questions into a checklist that expands any idea in seven directions.

Substitute: Replace a component

Combine: Merge two features

Adapt: Reuse in new context

Modify: Change scale or shape

Put to another use: New application

Eliminate: Remove complexity

Reverse: Flip the perspective

Try each letter on your top ideas. Could you substitute the API? Could you drop the most complex step?

#### Random Input

Maltese physician and author Edward de Bono, who coined [lateral thinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lateral_thinking), devised this technique for provoking new connections with randomness, set out in Serious Creativity (1992).

1.  Pick a random noun: open a dictionary, use a generator, or point at something in the room
2.  Force-connect it to your problem. How is your idea like a “lighthouse”?
3.  Follow the links that come up, even the weird ones

Let the word be random. If you pick one that already relates to your problem, you'll get the same ideas back.

#### Antidisciplinary Thinking

Joi Ito, then director of the MIT Media Lab, on finding innovation in the white space between fields, from his 2014 essay [Antidisciplinary](https://joi.ito.com/weblog/2014/10/02/antidisciplinar.html).

Interdisciplinary work has existing fields collaborate. Antidisciplinary work fits none of them, so don't drop an idea just because you can't say which field it belongs to. “An AI 911 dispatcher?” sounded strange until DispatchAI won the Grand Prize.

Ask Yourself

-   Which field would never normally work on this problem?
-   What would a musician, a chef, or a game designer do with it?

> “Creativity is just connecting things. …  
> The broader one's understanding of the human experience, the better design we will have.”

— Steve Jobs, co-founder of Apple, [Wired interview, 1996](https://www.wired.com/1996/02/jobs-2/)

## Ideation Checklist

If you're competing to win, finish this before the event. If you're there to learn, you can run it at the event with the people you meet.

TL;DR Combine 20-30 elements into 15-20 candidates, then pick one your team can ship in the time you have.

Build a list of 20-30+ tech, domain, and wild card elements

Combine at random without filtering. Aim for 15-20 combinations before you judge any

Take a break and jot down what comes to you

Run SCAMPER on your top 3 ideas, all 7 letters

Favor ideas that use something that just became possible

Validate with your team: does it excite everyone, and can you ship it in 24-48 hours?
